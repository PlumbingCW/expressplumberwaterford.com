How to Install Outdoor Shower Plumbing in Waterford, CT

An outdoor shower is a simple idea with a few details that matter, especially drainage and winter protection. Here is how to plan one.

☎ Call (860) 397-8103See Services

By the Waterford Express Plumbing team · Published 2026-10-16 · Updated 2026-11-16

Plan the drain before the fixtures

An outdoor shower needs three things: a water supply, a way to control temperature and a place for the used water to go. The supply is the easy part. Drainage and winter protection are where projects go wrong.

In a cold climate, the shower must also be drained or shut off before freezing weather. A line left full of water can split silently and cause a leak when it thaws.

The basic parts

What a typical outdoor shower includes.

  • A hot and cold supply from inside the house, with shutoff valves.
  • A mixing valve and a shower head rated for outdoor use.
  • Supports and a wall or post that can carry the weight.
  • Piping that slopes to a drain point for winter.
  • A drainage plan, such as a gravel bed or a connection to approved drainage.

Supply and winter protection

Run the supply from a point inside the house where you can shut it off and drain it. Install the shutoff valves with a drain valve on the outdoor side so you can empty the pipe in fall.

Use materials made for outdoor use, and slope the piping so water runs out when the valve is opened. A trapped section of pipe can freeze and split, even if the rest of the line is empty.

If you want hot water, plan the run from the water heater. Long pipe runs lose heat and make the user wait, so consider where the shower sits relative to the heater. Our plumbing installation page explains how we scope a project like this.

Costs and planning questions

The price of an outdoor shower depends on the supply run, the drainage method and the enclosure. A shower on the side of a house near a bathroom is simple. One at the end of a long yard needs trenching and more pipe.

Ask a plumber for a written scope that lists the supply, the shutoff and drain valves, the drainage method and the permit. Compare quotes for the same work, since some include the enclosure and others do not.

Using the shower safely

Safety matters more outdoors.

  • Use ground fault protection for any nearby outlet or light.
  • Keep the floor slip resistant and drained.
  • Keep the water temperature safe with a mixing valve.
  • Close the shutoff in the fall and open the drain valve.
  • Check supports and fasteners each spring.
  • Keep soap runoff away from wells and streams.

Materials and placement

Choose a spot near an existing supply line, with a view that suits your privacy. A wall of the house, a fence or a purpose built enclosure all work. Keep the shower away from siding that can rot and from electrical outlets.

Use stainless steel or brass fixtures that stand up to sun, salt air and rain. Plastic parts exposed to sunlight can crack. Wrap exposed pipes with insulation that is rated for outdoor use.

Hot water for an outdoor shower

Running a hot line from the water heater is the most comfortable choice, but the pipe run can be long. A longer run means more wait for hot water and more heat lost in the pipe. Insulating the line and keeping the run short improves both.

Some owners use a small point of use heater, while others rely on a cold only shower for summer. Each choice changes the plumbing, so decide before the walls and trenches are done.

Seasonal care and safety

Keep the shower safe and ready each year.

  • Use a slip resistant floor surface.
  • Keep electrical devices well away from the spray.
  • Drain the line before the first freeze.
  • Check fittings for leaks each spring.
  • Use a vacuum breaker or check valve if the code requires one.

Soap, shampoo and the ground

Soap and shampoo can harm plants and soil if they drain to the same spot every day. Choose biodegradable products and spread drainage over a wider area where rules allow it.

Do not send shower water to a storm drain or a stream. That may be prohibited, and it harms water quality. Ask the town before you choose a drainage method.

Waterford note: shoreline, rules and permits

More than 11 of Waterford's 44.6 square miles are water, and many homes sit close to the shore, rivers or wetlands. Near the water, drainage choices are more tightly watched, so ask the town before you drain to the ground.

The town's Building Department says permits are required before new construction, additions, renovations or improvements. New plumbing lines usually count, so ask what your outdoor shower needs.

Waterford is served by the New London water system, so the shutoff and supply questions go to the utility if you want to tap into the main side. For lines inside your property, a licensed plumber handles it.

The 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), adopted by the Connecticut State Building Code according to the state, requires backflow protection on outdoor fixtures, which is why a vacuum breaker is part of the plan.

Closing it up each fall

A fall routine protects the plumbing.

  1. Turn off the supply valve inside the house.
  2. Open the outdoor drain valve and the shower valve to empty the line.
  3. Check that the pipe is dry and that water is not trapped in a low spot.
  4. Cover or remove the fixtures if they are not winter rated.
  5. Reverse the steps in spring and check for leaks.
