Hydro Jetting a Sewer Line: A Waterford, CT Homeowner Guide

Hydro jetting uses high pressure water to scour a sewer line clean. Here is when it helps, when it does not and what to ask before you book it.

☎ Call (860) 397-8103See Services

By the Waterford Express Plumbing team · Published 2026-09-14 · Updated 2026-11-16

The short answer on hydro jetting

Hydro jetting is a way to clean a drain line by blasting it with high pressure water from a hose and nozzle. It removes grease, scale, sludge and many roots, and it leaves the pipe walls cleaner than a cable alone. It works best on a line that is structurally sound but coated with buildup.

It is not the right tool for every backup. A cracked, collapsed or badly offset pipe needs a camera and a repair plan first, because water pressure applied to a broken line can make the damage worse. A good plumber looks before jetting.

How hydro jetting works

A hose with a special nozzle is fed into the line through a cleanout. Jets pointed backward pull the hose forward while jets pointed outward scrub the walls of the pipe. Debris is flushed toward the main sewer or the septic tank, depending on your system.

The operator controls pressure for the pipe material and size. A cast iron line with scale takes a different setting from a plastic line with grease, and a thin walled old pipe may not be a candidate at all.

Compare that with cabling. A cable punches a hole through a blockage, which clears the water but can leave a coating on the walls. The line may flow again and then clog again in a few months because the buildup is still there.

When jetting makes sense

These are the situations where jetting often earns its cost.

  • Repeated backups in the same line after cabling.
  • Grease buildup in a kitchen or restaurant line.
  • Heavy scale or sludge shown on a camera inspection.
  • Fine root growth that has not yet split the pipe.
  • Routine maintenance on a commercial drain on a schedule.

When to say no to jetting

If a camera shows a broken joint, a collapsed section or a belly where water sits, jetting does not fix the shape of the pipe. It may clean the debris and then leave you with the same backup next month.

Very old clay or cast iron pipes can be fragile. If the plumber cannot see the line first, ask for a camera look before any high pressure work. See our page on sewer inspection for what that visit shows.

If roots have grown into the line, jetting can cut them back, but they usually return. The longer term fix may be a repair or replacement of the affected section, covered on sewer line repair.

What the process looks like on site

The plumber starts by locating the cleanout, which is the capped pipe that gives access to the sewer line. In many homes it sits in the basement, the garage or the yard near the foundation. If you cannot find it, tell the dispatcher, because finding access is part of the visit.

Next comes a camera run if the line allows it. The footage shows scale, grease, roots and any cracks or sags. With that picture, the operator picks a nozzle and sets the pressure for the pipe material, then works from the cleanout toward the main in passes.

Afterward, a second camera run confirms the result. You should see clean pipe walls and a clear path for water. Ask for a short summary of what was found, where and at what distance, so you can compare it with any later inspection.

Signs you may need it

Slow drains in several fixtures, gurgling toilets and a bad smell near a floor drain all point to a restriction in a shared line. If a cable cleared the problem only to see it return within months, buildup is probably coating the walls.

Kitchen lines in homes that cook often are common candidates, since grease cools and sticks as it moves away from the sink. Older cast iron lines may carry rough scale that catches paper and debris. In both cases, jetting scours what a cable only punctures.

Waterford note: sewer, septic and permits

Before you book any work on the line, find out what the line connects to. Some properties in town are on public sewer and some use private septic systems, and the town can tell you which applies to your address.

If a jetting visit leads to a repair, the Waterford Building Department states that permits are required before new construction, additions, renovations or improvements, and it accepts applications in person or through its online portal. Ask what a sewer repair needs before work starts.

Waterford covers about 44.6 square miles, and more than 11 of them are water. That is a reminder that low lying properties near the shore and the Niantic River deal with high groundwater, which can put extra load on aging lines in wet seasons.

According to the Connecticut Office of the State Building Inspector, the 2022 state building code adopts the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC), which governs any sewer repair that follows a camera inspection.

What to ask before you book

A short checklist keeps the visit useful.

  1. Ask whether the plumber will run a camera first and share the footage.
  2. Ask which pressure and nozzle they plan to use for your pipe material.
  3. Confirm the price in writing before work starts.
  4. Ask what happens if the camera shows damage.
  5. Ask for a short summary afterward with before and after notes.
