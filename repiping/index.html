When patching stops working

Repiping in Waterford, CT is a planned project that replaces the water supply piping in a house. It makes sense when leaks keep returning, water pressure is poor because pipes have narrowed or water comes out discolored from corroded lines.

Most homes can be repiped in a few days with careful planning. The work is easier on a family when you know the steps and the scope in advance, which is what this page lays out.

Signs that repiping is the real fix

One leak is a repair. A pattern is a warning. These are the signs we look for.

  • Pinhole leaks or pipe failures recurring in different places.
  • Low pressure that has worsened over time, especially at upstairs fixtures.
  • Rust-colored or metallic-tasting water that clears slowly.
  • Visible corrosion or white mineral crust on exposed pipes.
  • Galvanized steel or polybutylene piping, which have known aging problems.
  • Insurance note: pipe replacement is generally a homeowner expense, but some insurers may charge more or decline coverage on certain old materials, so ask yours.

How to tell what pipe you have

Waterford's Utility Commission describes a simple check for the service line entering your house near the meter. Copper looks orange and is not magnetic. Plastic is blue, white or black and not magnetic. Galvanized steel is hard to scratch and a magnet sticks. A soft, easily scratched gray pipe that a magnet does not stick to could be lead, and the utility asks homeowners to report it.

Inside the house, the same tests apply to exposed pipes in the basement or crawlspace. Gray flexible plastic could be polybutylene, which is known for failure. Call us to confirm before you spend money on an assumption.

Leaks keep coming back?

Call and describe the pattern. We will help you decide whether repair or repiping is the better value, and we can take a first look through leak detection.

Copper versus PEX

Both are common. The right choice depends on the house and your priorities.

FactorCopperPEX
InstallationMore labor, soldered jointsFlexible, fewer fittings, faster.
LifespanVery long when water is not aggressiveLong, but sensitive to UV and certain chemicals.
Wall impactMore openings neededFewer openings.
CostTypically higherTypically lower.

How a repipe project runs

A repipe is a planned sequence, not a surprise.

  1. We inspect, map the existing lines and recommend a material and route.
  2. You receive a written scope and price before any wall is opened.
  3. The old lines are shut off and replaced in sections, with water restored daily when possible.
  4. Small access holes are patched, and we test every joint under pressure.
  5. You receive a closeout summary and any permit paperwork required.

Permits and licensed work

A repipe is major plumbing work. In Connecticut, plumbing contractors are licensed through the Department of Consumer Protection, and the eLicense lookup at elicense.ct.gov shows whether a P-1 license is active.

Waterford requires permits before improvements, according to the Building Department. Ask what your project needs. We can describe the scope so the question is easy to answer.

If you are already planning a remodel, repiping at the same time saves labor because walls are open. See plumbing installation for related work.

Living in the house during a repipe

Most families stay home. We plan shutoffs for short blocks of time and restore water at the end of each day when possible, so cooking and bathing are not interrupted for long.

Expect some dust and noise. We cover floors, protect furniture and clean up daily. Tell us about pets, work from home schedules and anyone with medical needs so we can plan around them.

Code and permit note

A repipe is major work. According to the Connecticut Office of the State Building Inspector, the 2022 state building code adopts the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC). Approved pipe, support spacing and pressure tests all come from that standard, and we plan the job around them.

What changes the price

Repiping prices depend on the house and the materials.

  • Home size, number of bathrooms and floors.
  • Pipe material chosen and the route through the house.
  • Wall and ceiling access and repairs after.
  • Whether the water service line also needs work, see water service line repair.
  • Permits and inspections.
  • Phasing the work so the family can stay in the home.
